## The Sign and Judgment Ministry of Peter in the Book of Acts: A Reformed Redemptive-Historical Pericope Survey

The miracle ministry of the Apostle Peter in the book of Acts represents a crucial redemptive-historical link between the earthly ministry of Jesus Christ and the establishment of the early Church. Far from being random acts of philanthropy or localized demonstrations of supernatural power, these signs and judgments serve to validate apostolic authority, define the boundary markers of the new covenant community, and signal the geographic expansion of the gospel from Jerusalem into Judea and Samaria.

## Acts 3:1–10: The Healing of the Lame Beggar at the Gate Beautiful

### Theologian Publications and Exegetical Focus

The healing of the congenital paralytic at the temple entrance represents Peter’s initial public sign. R.C. Sproul treats this pericope directly in his sermon Healing at the Gate Beautiful, focusing on the transition from Israel's old covenant worship to the new covenant church.[^1][^3] John MacArthur addresses the passage in his exposition God's Purpose for Miracles, highlighting how signs serve to authenticate the spoken word.[^9] John Piper expounds on this text in his message Jesus' Name Made This Man Strong, emphasizing that the power of the ascended Christ remains active on earth through faith in His name.[^16] Sinclair Ferguson expounds on the passage by highlighting how Peter's subsequent sermon links the healing to Christ's ascension and coronation in He Ascended into Heaven [^21], and how it reveals the goodness of God's character in Times of Refreshing.[^22] Kevin DeYoung highlights the cosmic irony of the healing in Wonder of Wonders [^34] and A Sermon for Those Who Killed Jesus.[^35] Alastair Begg expounds upon this pericope in The Transforming Power of Christ to define the unique spiritual mission of the Church over and against material philanthropy.[^37][^38] Charles Spurgeon addresses the text in multiple sermons, including Apostolic Exhortation, The Power of Christ's Name, and The Prince of Life, isolating the power of the name of Jesus to raise spiritually paralyzed sinners.[^40][^41][^42][^43] No direct published works on this specific pericope by J.I. Packer, D.A. Carson, Joel Beeke, or Michael Horton were identified in the target source materials.

### Word Studies and Exegetical Nuance

Charles Spurgeon focuses extensively on the christological weight of the phrase "In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth" (Acts 3:6).[^42] He translates "Jesus Christ" into English as "Saviour anointed".[^44] He notes that "Nazareth" is an explicit marker of Christ's humiliation.[^42] This title links Christ to His lowliness as the "carpenter's son" laboring in Nazareth.[^42] Spurgeon observes that "because of that very descent, God has highly exalted him".[^42] Consequently, the name that represented localized weakness is revealed to contain absolute, cosmic authority capable of strengthening congenital birth-lameness.[^42]

Spurgeon also conducts a detailed lexical distinction between "repentance" and "conversion" in his treatment of Peter's explanatory sermon (Acts 3:19).[^40][^41] Repentance is defined as "a discovery of the evil of sin, a mourning that we have committed it, a resolution to forsake it".[^40][^41] It is a profound, internal change of mind where a person "loves what once he hated, and hates what once he loved".[^40][^41] Conversion is translated as "a turning round... from sin to holiness, from carelessness to thought, from the world to heaven, from self to Jesus".[^40][^41] Spurgeon uses a series of biblical analogies to clarify this: the casting out of demons represents repentance, while putting on clothes and being in one's right mind represents conversion.[^40][^41] The prodigal coming to himself in the pigsty represents repentance, while his physical journey back to his father's house represents conversion.[^40][^41]

John Piper outlines three periods of time in Acts 3:19–23, analyzing the prophetic timeline.[^45] The first period covers "the days of Jesus' earthly life and ministry," where God raised up a new prophet like Moses under Deuteronomy 18:15.[^45] This timeline demonstrates that the miracles of Christ’s earthly life were the ultimate sending of the Prophet to turn Israel first from its wickedness.[^45]

### Historical and Cultural Background

The narrative setting of the miracle is highly significant. R.C. Sproul, Charles Spurgeon, and Alastair Begg explain the timing—the "ninth hour" or 3:00 p.m., which was the traditional Jewish hour of prayer and evening sacrifice.[^37][^40][^46] John MacArthur and Alastair Begg on the social role of the "Gate Beautiful" as a high-traffic center for almsgiving.[^14][^38] Secular and religious custom dictated that worshippers entering the temple were expected to perform "almsdeeds" as a regular expression of piety.[^37][^38] The lame beggar was carried there daily, fully dependent on this system of religious charity for basic physical survival.[^37][^38] His initial expectation of Peter and John was entirely financial, illustrating a complete lack of spiritual anticipation.[^14][^38]

### Old Testament Typology and Cross-References

Sinclair Ferguson connects the healing and subsequent sermon in Acts 3 to the fulfillment of Psalm 24:3–6.[^21][^25] He identifies Christ as the "Lead Climber" and the ultimate "Hero of Faith" who has ascended the holy hill of the Lord with clean hands and a pure heart.[^21][^25] The restoration of the lame beggar is thus presented as a physical, earthly manifestation of Christ's triumph and coronation in the heavenly places.[^21] Kevin DeYoung highlights Peter's use of Deuteronomy 18:15 to identify Jesus as the long-expected "Prophet like Moses" whom Israel was commanded to obey on pain of covenantal excision.[^35][^47] Sproul notes that Peter's sermon immediately recalls Christ's prayer of execution: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do" (Luke 23:34), as Peter mellows his denunciation by stating they "acted in ignorance".[^46]

### Doctrinal Significance

This pericope establishes the theological purpose of the miraculous in the apostolic era. John MacArthur argues that miracles are "pointers" or "signs" designed to authenticate the apostolic office and the newly inaugurated message of the gospel.[^9][^14] Alastair Begg develops the doctrine of the Church's unique, spiritual mission over and against material philanthropy.[^38] He asserts that while the Church is called to engage in mercy ministry, it departs from its unique commission when it attempts to substitute material resources or social justice for the transforming power of the Holy Spirit.[^38] Charles Spurgeon addresses the divine monergism of regeneration, where the Holy Spirit must initiate spiritual life before any human response is possible, comparing the physical quickening of the beggar to the spiritual resurrection of a sinner.[^40][^41]

## Acts 5:1–11: The Covenantal Judgment of Ananias and Sapphira

### Theologian Publications and Exegetical Focus

The sudden, lethal judgment of Ananias and Sapphira stands in stark contrast to the preceding narratives of healing. R.C. Sproul treats this passage as a crucial demonstration of the holiness of God, the personality of the Holy Spirit, and the relationship between divine justice and grace in Ananias and Sapphira's Hypocrisy, God the Holy Spirit, and Holiness and Justice.[^4][^5][^6][^7][^48] John MacArthur addresses the text within the context of the "sin leading to death," church discipline, and the pursuit of holiness in The Sin Leading to Death and The Sin Unto Death.[^10][^11][^12] John Piper compares the duplicity of Ananias and Sapphira with the authentic generosity of Barnabas in his sermon Be Like Barnabas Not Like Ananias!.[^17] Sinclair Ferguson utilizes the text to defend the full deity and personal nature of the Holy Spirit in I Believe in the Holy Spirit.[^24] D.A. Carson offers a comprehensive commentary and sermon on this pericope, focusing on the preservation of holiness during periods of intense spiritual revival in Revival and Judgment.[^27] Joel Beeke analyzes the passage in his treatments of church discipline and the persistent danger of religious hypocrisy in Discipline of God [^31] and Hypocrisy.[^30] Michael Horton connects the text to the doctrine of repentance, defining it as a sovereign gift that redirects the soul away from self-glorification in The Gift of Repentance.[^32] Kevin DeYoung traces the ecclesiological shifts marked by this judgment in The Honeymoon Is Over.[^36] J.I. Packer, Alastair Begg, and Charles Spurgeon have no direct published works on this specific pericope preserved in the target source materials.

### Word Studies and Exegetical Nuance

R.C. Sproul and Joel Beeke highlight the theological weight of the Greek verb used in Acts 5:2 and 5:3: nosphizomai, translated as "kept back" or "embezzled".[^6][^30] Sproul notes that this exact term is utilized in the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) in Joshua 7:1 to describe the sin of Achan, who "kept back" a portion of the spoils of Jericho that had been devoted entirely to destruction (herem) for the Lord.[^6] This verbal link establishes a direct covenantal parallel between Achan’s theft at the inception of Israel's possession of the Promised Land and Ananias’s fraud at the inception of the New Testament Church.[^6]

D.A. Carson focuses on the grammatical and historical implications of the word "conspiracy" or "agreed together" (symphoneo) in Acts 5:9.[^27][^49] Carson notes that their sin was not a sudden, individual lapse into greed, but a premeditated, shared decision to test the Holy Spirit.[^27] This corporate, conscious agreement to present a partial gift as a complete sacrifice demonstrated a unified defiance of the Holy Spirit's oversight of the community.[^27]

Sinclair Ferguson highlights that the Old Testament word for spirit, ruach, is onomatopoeic, echoing the sound of wind in motion or storm-wind.[^24] He notes how the Greek text of Acts 5:3–4 demonstrates the Spirit's personality and deity, as the Holy Spirit is treated as a personal entity who can be lied to and who possesses full divine attributes.[^24]

Michael Horton defines repentance in this context as "the revulsion of the whole soul toward its alliance with sin and death".[^32] He explains that true repentance is a sovereign gift (Acts 5:31) where the Holy Spirit turns the sinner outward from the self (incurvatus in se) toward the risen Christ, which is the direct opposite of the self-glorifying path chosen by Ananias and Sapphira.[^32]

### Historical and Cultural Background

Carson, MacArthur, and Piper contextualize the narrative by contrasting Ananias and Sapphira with Barnabas (Acts 4:36–37).[^27][^30][^50] In the early Jerusalem congregation, believers voluntarily sold land and houses to provide for the destitute.[^36][^50] Carson and MacArthur emphasize that this sharing of goods was entirely voluntary; there was no compulsory, communal mandate.[^27][^30][^51] Peter explicitly confirms this: the property belonged to Ananias before it was sold, and the proceeds were entirely at his disposal (Acts 5:4).[^30][^49][^51] The cultural problem arose because Barnabas and others had received significant honor, prestige, and "moral and spiritual kudos" within the community for their sacrificial giving.[^27][^30] Ananias and Sapphira coveted this spiritual status but were unwilling to pay the actual cost.[^27][^30] Their hypocrisy was a calculated attempt to purchase a reputation for high-level sanctity while privately retaining their wealth.[^27][^30]

### Old Testament Typology and Cross-References

Sproul, Beeke, and DeYoung build a strong typological connection to the narrative of Achan in Joshua 7.[^6][^36] In both accounts:

- A major covenantal transition is taking place (the conquest of Canaan; the birth of the church).[^6]
- A representative individual "keeps back" (nosphizomai) what belongs to God, attempting to conceal the theft.[^6]
- The hidden sin threatens the spiritual vitality and witness of the entire covenant community.[^6]
- God halts His standard pattern of administrative patience and executes swift, terminal judgment on the offenders to preserve the purity of His people.[^6]

Sproul also connects Peter's confrontation of Ananias to the pattern of prophetic confrontation seen in the Old Testament, particularly Nathan’s indictment of David in 2 Samuel 12.[^6] He notes that David's ultimate confession, "Against you, you only, have I sinned" (Psalm 51:4), mirrors Peter's theological assertion that Ananias had "not lied to man but to God" (Acts 5:4).[^6]

### Doctrinal Significance

This pericope is a primary text for several foundational doctrines in Reformed theology:

### The Deity and Personality of the Holy Spirit

Sproul and Ferguson argue that Acts 5:3–4 is one of the most explicit declarations of Trinitarian theology in the New Testament.[^7][^24][^48] Peter interchanges "lying to the Holy Spirit" (v. 3) with "lying to God" (v. 4).[^7][^24][^48] This grammatical parallel proves that the Holy Spirit is not an impersonal force or "divine liquid," but is Himself fully God, sharing the identical divine essence while existing as a distinct person within the Godhead.[^7][^24][^48]

### Divine Holiness, Justice, and Grace

Sproul expounds on this text in his seminal work on the holiness of God, addressing the modern objection that the terminal punishment of Ananias and Sapphira far outweighed their crime.[^5] Sproul writes that when God executes immediate justice, it is not an act of cruelty, but a revelation of His holy standard.[^5] The striking dead of these hypocrites is a stark reminder that physical death is the natural, deserved consequence of all sin (Romans 6:23).[^6] Sproul explains that "God interrupts His normal pattern of mercy to remind His people that they do not deserve it".[^6] It is a warning against taking common grace and covenantal patience for granted.[^5]

### The Necessity of Ecclesiastical Discipline

Carson, Beeke, and MacArthur identify this judgment as the supreme biblical validation for church discipline.[^13][^27][^31] Carson notes that "in times of genuine revival, judgment may be more immediate than in times of decay".[^27] When God allows a decaying church's sins to multiply without consequence, it is the "worst judgment of all" because it leads to total spiritual erosion.[^27] Prompt, severe discipline, however, infuses a healthy fear of God into the church, preserving its doctrinal and moral integrity.[^13][^27][^31]

### Theological Agonies and the Soteriological Status of the Offenders

The most significant theological disagreement among these Reformed theologians concerns the eternal state of Ananias and Sapphira.

- **The Regenerate/Believer View**: John MacArthur represents the view that Ananias and Sapphira were genuine, regenerate believers who fell into severe, flagrant sin.[^10][^11] MacArthur argues that their story is deliberately set within the context of the actions of "all the believers" (Acts 4:32).[^50] He identifies their sudden deaths as the premier biblical example of the "sin leading to physical death" (1 John 5:16).[^10][^11][^12] MacArthur explains: "Because they were believers, the Lord took them to be with Himself, but He could not allow such wickedness to corrupt the church".[^11] In this view, their execution was a severe act of paternal chastisement to protect the purity of the church, but their souls were eternally saved.[^10][^11]
- **The Unregenerate/False Professor View**: R.C. Sproul and D.A. Carson are far more cautious, presenting arguments that suggest Ananias and Sapphira may have been unregenerate hypocrites who lacked saving faith.[^6][^27] Sproul writes that their "malicious deception... often reveals a lack of genuine faith".[^6] He notes that the divine gift of saving faith is what ultimately distinguishes those who receive mercy from those who receive wrath, implying that Ananias and Sapphira received the wrath of external judgment because they lacked this foundational gift.[^6] Carson describes their sin as "a blatant lie against the Holy Spirit" that betrayed "a stance so removed from the God-centeredness of genuine faith that it was idolatrous".[^27] Carson argues that they coveted the reputation of self-denial without possessing the actual reality, indicating a complete absence of the internal, regenerating work of the Spirit.[^27]

## Acts 5:15–16: The Shadow of Peter and the Multiplied Healings of Jerusalem

### Theologian Publications and Exegetical Focus

These transitional verses record the peak of Peter’s public healing ministry. John MacArthur addresses this pericope to dissect the mechanics of apostolic miracles and correct popular superstitions regarding Peter's shadow in Every Day in the Temple.[^14] John Piper explores how these signs and wonders served as a powerful instrument to draw multitudes to the gospel in How Signs and Wonders Helped Add Multitudes to the Lord.[^17][^18] Kevin DeYoung highlights the structural shifts in this passage in The Honeymoon Is Over, pointing out that this is the moment where Luke introduces the term "church" to describe the unified body.[^36] R.C. Sproul, J.I. Packer, Sinclair Ferguson, D.A. Carson, Joel Beeke, Michael Horton, Alastair Begg, and Charles Spurgeon do not have direct, specific publications on this pericope preserved in the target source documents.

### Historical and Cultural Background

John MacArthur provides essential cultural background concerning the popular belief in "shadows" in the first-century Near East.[^14] He explains that in many ancient cultures, it was widely believed that a individual's shadow carried their personal influence, power, and character.[^14] Consequently, parents would intentionally place their children within the physical shadow of a respected, powerful person to receive their blessing, or would frantically snatch them away from the shadow of someone they disliked or feared.[^14]
MacArthur highlights an important exegetical distinction: the biblical text does not state that Peter's shadow actually healed anyone.[^14] It merely reports that the people believed it could, and therefore laid their sick in the streets so that "at least his shadow might fall on some of them" (Acts 5:15).[^14][^49] While the populace may have held a culturally conditioned superstition, God accommodated their weak faith by performing extraordinary, comprehensive miracles through the presence and ministry of the apostles.[^14]

### Doctrinal Significance

The primary doctrinal focus of these verses is the authentication of foundational apostolic authority. MacArthur points out that the healing was absolute and universal: "they were all healed" (Acts 5:16), with "no failures, no lack of power".[^14] This is contrasted with modern faith healers, whose efforts are characterized by high failure rates and excuses blaming a lack of faith in the recipient.[^14] MacArthur notes that this unqualified, sovereign healing power was restricted to the apostolic era to serve as the "signs of a true apostle" (2 Corinthians 12:12), authenticating the foundational generation of the Church.[^14]

Additionally, Kevin DeYoung notes that in the immediate aftermath of this event (Acts 5:11), Luke utilizes the Greek word ekklesia ("church") for the first time in the narrative.[^36] This represents a significant doctrinal transition: the community is no longer described merely as a localized group of "believers" or "disciples," but as a formal, covenantal assembly—the visible body of Jesus Christ, marked by both a terrifying holiness and a magnetic, converting power.[^13][^36]

## Acts 9:32–35: The Paralytic Healed—The Recovery of Aeneas at Lydda

### Theologian Publications and Exegetical Focus

The healing of Aeneas at Lydda marks the transition of Peter's ministry from the immediate environment of Jerusalem to the broader regions of Judea and Sharon. Charles Spurgeon preached a comprehensive sermon on this passage, Aeneas, extracting rich evangelical and pastoral lessons from Aeneas's paralysis and subsequent instantaneous cure.[^44] Alastair Begg provides an exposition of this healing in his study The Healing of Aeneas, framing it within the geographical expansion of the early Church during a temporary season of peace.[^39] R.C. Sproul's Ligonier platform addresses this transition in the devotional outline The Spread of the Gospel.[^8] John MacArthur, John Piper, J.I. Packer, Sinclair Ferguson, D.A. Carson, Joel Beeke, Michael Horton, and Kevin DeYoung do not have direct, specific publications on this pericope preserved in the target source materials.

### Word Studies and Exegetical Nuance

Charles Spurgeon engages in a comparative name study of the paralytic, "Aeneas".[^44] He contrasts this biblical figure with the classic hero of Roman literature, Virgil’s Aeneas.[^44] Spurgeon remarks that while this man bears a "classic name," he is "no mighty warrior," but rather a poor, paralyzed beggar.[^44] He notes that the Christian reader is "not to hear Virgil sing, 'arms and the man,' but we are to let Luke tell us of the man and his Saviour".[^44]

Spurgeon also analyzes the theological name "Jesus Christ" as pronounced by Peter in his declaration, "Aeneas, Jesus Christ heals you" (Acts 9:34).[^39][^44] He notes that "Jesus Christ" translated into English means a "Saviour anointed".[^44] The proclamation is not a magic formula but a declaration of the immediate, active, and sovereign authority of the ascended, anointed Messiah.[^44]

### Historical and Cultural Background

The historical geography of Acts 9 is crucial. Alastair Begg notes that this event takes place during a distinct historical interlude described in Acts 9:31, where the church throughout Judea, Galilee, and Samaria experienced a period of peace, edification, and growth.[^52] Peter is described as traveling "here and there among them all" (Acts 9:32), acting as an itinerant apostolic supervisor.[^39] He comes down to "the saints who lived at Lydda," which was located about twenty miles west of Jerusalem on the road to the Mediterranean coast.[^8] Aeneas had been paralyzed and bedridden for eight years.[^39][^44] Spurgeon highlights the cultural and physical hopelessness of his condition: in the ancient world, a paralytic of eight years was deemed entirely incurable by any human physician.[^44] He was a complete burden to his family and community, possessing no economic or social utility.[^44]

### Old Testament Typology and Cross-References

Charles Spurgeon draws a powerful typological parallel between the complete eradication of Aeneas's paralysis and the Old Testament deliverance of Israel at the Red Sea (Exodus 14–15).[^44] He recalls how the Israelites were initially terrified when pursued by Pharaoh’s hosts, but after God's sovereign intervention, Miriam and the daughters of Israel could sing, "The depths have covered them: there is not one of them left".[^44] Spurgeon applies this to the doctrine of justification: when a sinner is healed by Christ, "the whole army of your sins shall sink beneath the sea of his blood," leaving the soul justified and entirely free.[^44]

### Doctrinal Significance

Spurgeon utilizes Aeneas's physical paralysis as a multi-layered metaphor to establish several core Reformed doctrines:

#### Total Depravity and Spiritual Inability

Spurgeon argues that Aeneas's physical condition is a picture of the sinner's spiritual state.[^44] Aeneas was "bereaved of all strength," unable to move his hands or feet, completely helpless.[^44] Similarly, the unregenerate human soul is spiritually paralyzed, completely unable to perform any saving good, repent, or believe apart from the sovereign, quickening grace of God.[^44] Spurgeon asserts: "as there is no room for a great cure unless there is a great sickness, so there is no room for God’s great grace unless there is great sin".[^44]

#### Instantaneous Regeneration

Spurgeon emphasizes that Aeneas’s healing was immediate.[^44] Peter did not prescribe a gradual course of physical therapy; he commanded him to "rise and make your bed," and "immediately he rose".[^39][^44] This physical immediacy mirrors the spiritual reality of regeneration: the Holy Spirit does not gradually regenerate a heart, but instantaneously imparts spiritual life to the dead soul.[^44] This instantaneous transformation is inevitably evidenced by immediate obedience.[^44]

## Acts 9:36–42: The Raising of Tabitha (Dorcas) at Joppa

### Theologian Publications and Exegetical Focus

The raising of Tabitha (Dorcas) at Joppa represents one of the most remarkable miracles in the New Testament, being one of the few recorded instances of an apostle raising the dead. John MacArthur expounds on this text within his study of diaconal ministry and the church’s responsibility to care for widows in Caring for Widows.[^15] Sinclair Ferguson addresses this narrative in devotional studies, The Raising of Dorcas and Tabitha, Full of Good Works, highlighting Tabitha as a model of Christian discipleship and good works.[^26] Michael Horton utilizes this pericope to explore the theological relationship between the church as Christ's physical body and its mandate for mercy ministry in Mercy Ministry and Social Justice.[^33] R.C. Sproul, John Piper, J.I. Packer, D.A. Carson, Joel Beeke, Kevin DeYoung, Alastair Begg, and Charles Spurgeon do not have direct, specific commentaries on this pericope in the target source materials.

### Historical and Cultural Background

Joppa was an ancient seaport located on the Mediterranean coast, approximately twelve miles northwest of Lydda.[^8] John MacArthur provides detailed historical background on first-century Jewish burial customs.[^15] Unlike the Romans or Egyptians, Jews did not practice embalming.[^15] Instead, their immediate custom upon death was to thoroughly wash the corpse.[^15] This was followed by a period of immediate viewing and mourning, with burial typically occurring within twenty-four hours.[^15]

MacArthur also explains the social vulnerability of widows in first-century Jewish and Roman societies.[^15] Widows lacked legal representation, financial independence, and social status; without a male relative to advocate for them, they were frequently reduced to absolute destitution.[^15] Tabitha, whose Greek name Dorcas means "Gazelle," had dedicated her life and personal resources to weaving chitons (inner tunics) and himations (outer cloaks) to clothe these vulnerable women.[^15] The weeping widows who surrounded Peter, showing him the garments she had made, were presenting physical evidence of her vital, indispensable role in their survival.[^15]

### Doctrinal Significance

The doctrinal insights derived from this pericope focus on the corporate nature of the church and the reality of resurrection power:

#### The Church as the Visible Body of Christ

Michael Horton develops a robust ecclesiological doctrine from this text.[^33] He argues that the Church is not merely an invisible, mystical entity—"Christ's soul"—but is His physical, visible organization and society on earth.[^33] Because believers are united to Christ by the Holy Spirit, they are necessarily bound to one another, "feeling one another's aches and pains as part of our own flesh".[^33] Tabitha's diaconal work was not a secular philanthropic hobby but a vital outworking of the church's covenantal obligation to care for the physical needs of the saints.[^33] Horton connects this to the reality of the final judgment: Christ identifies so closely with His needy people that what is done for "the least of these my brothers" is done directly to Him.[^33] This bond is so inextricable that when the ascended Jesus appeared to Saul on the Damascus road, He asked, "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" (Acts 9:4–5), showing that Christ suffers with His body.[^33]

#### Resurrection Power and Apostolic Validation

MacArthur and Ferguson note that Peter’s action in Joppa mirrors the raising of Jairus’s daughter by Jesus in Mark 5:35–43.[^15] Just as Jesus put the mockers and crowd out of the room, Peter clears the chamber of the weeping widows, kneels, and prays.[^15] His command, "Tabitha, arise," mirrors Christ's "Talitha, cumi".[^15] This structural and linguistic parallel establishes that the identical resurrection power that resided in Jesus Christ was now actively operating through His apostolic representatives, validating their message and demonstrating that Christ had conquered the "absolute barrier of death".[^15][^53]

## Common Modern Misconceptions and Moralistic Errors Warned Against

The target theologians identify several pervasive, contemporary errors in the interpretation and application of these narratives, warning modern readers against stripping the texts of their redemptive-historical and christological focus.

### The Seeker-Sensitive and User-Friendly Ecclesiology

Sproul and MacArthur issue a stinging critique of modern, seeker-sensitive church growth models, utilizing the judgment of Ananias and Sapphira as a direct refutation of this methodology.[^13][^30] They observe that contemporary pragmatic models insist that the church must be presented as entirely non-threatening, "user-friendly," and focused on therapeutic comfort to attract the unchurched.[^13][^30] However, the early church grew precisely because of "great fear" and a profound, terrifying awareness of God's holiness.[^13][^30] In Acts 5:13, the immediate result of the lethal judgment was that "none of the rest dared join them".[^13][^49] Casual, insincere, and self-serving seekers were frightened away from making a superficial commitment.[^13] Yet, paradoxically, "more than ever believers were added to the Lord" (Acts 5:14).[^49] Sproul and MacArthur argue that when the church recovers a transcendent, holy understanding of worship and pursues purity, the Lord Himself sovereignly adds true converts.[^13]

### The Humanitarian and Social Gospel Error

Begg, MacArthur, and Horton warn against the modern moralistic error of reducing the apostolic miracles to a mandate for secular social justice or humanitarian aid.[^14][^33][^38] They note that modern readers often look at Peter healing the lame beggar (Acts 3) or Dorcas clothing widows (Acts 9) and conclude that the primary mission of the church is economic redistribution, political advocacy, and social reform.[^33][^38] The theologians counter that while mercy ministry is an essential fruit of the gospel, it must never usurp the proclamation of the cross.[^33][^38] Begg stresses that if the church only offers material relief, it offers nothing that a secular, non-Christian charity cannot provide.[^38] The unique, non-negotiable treasure of the Church is the transforming power of Jesus Christ to reconcile sinful human beings to a holy God.[^38]

### The Hypocrisy of Appearances (Ananias's Sin Today)

Joel Beeke and D.A. Carson warn against the moralistic tendency to read the narrative of Ananias and Sapphira as a remote, historical curiosity, failing to recognize how easily the same sin is repeated in modern evangelical circles.[^27][^30] Beeke explains that "we share Ananias' sin... when we try to make others think we are more spiritual than we are".[^30] This occurs when a believer:

- Creates the public impression of possessing a disciplined prayer life when they are privately prayerless.[^30]
- Promotes an image of financial generosity while privately harboring a tight, covetous grip on wealth.[^30]
- Exhorts others to holiness or theological precision while secretly indulging in unmortified sin.[^30]

Carson notes that times of intense spiritual revival actually multiply the temptation toward this hypocrisy.[^27] Because holiness is highly valued in a revived community, individuals find it "more attractive to be thought holy than to be holy," leading to deceptive displays of piety.[^27]

## Cross-Pericope Synthesis: The Redemptive-Historical Progression

When viewed as an integrated set, these five pericopes reveal a deliberate geographical, theological, and covenantal progression, tracked by Sinclair Ferguson and Alastair Begg through the overarching structure of the book of Acts.[^8][^52] This progression directly aligns with the geographical mandate of Acts 1:8: "in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth."

First, the ministry begins at the very heart of the old covenant system—the temple gate in Jerusalem (Acts 3).[^37][^38] The healing of the lame beggar at the Beautiful Gate serves as a direct, public challenge to the Jewish religious establishment, proving that the ascended "Prince of life" is the sole source of restoration.[^37][^43]

Second, the focus shifts inward to the covenant community (Acts 5:1–11).[^6][^36] Before the gospel can be successfully launched out of Jerusalem, the newly formed covenant assembly must be purified of internal hypocrisy and deceit.[^36] The swift judgment of Ananias and Sapphira establishes that the Holy Spirit's presence demands absolute holiness, preparing the church for the intense spiritual warfare of its global mission.[^6][^13]

Third, with internal purity established, the apostolic ministry experiences an exponential increase in power and visibility (Acts 5:15–16).[^14][^36] The streets of Jerusalem become a theater of Christ's sovereign, unqualified healing power, drawing multitudes from the surrounding towns.[^14][^49]

Fourth, following the martyrdom of Stephen, the gospel breaks out of Jerusalem.[^8] Peter travels west down the Judean hills to Lydda (Acts 9:32–35).[^8] The healing of the paralytic Aeneas demonstrates that Christ's active, regenerating power is not localized to Jerusalem or the temple, but is fully operational throughout the coastal plains of Sharon, resulting in the conversion of entire cities.[^8][^44]

Fifth, Peter reaches Joppa at the edge of the Mediterranean Sea—the literal boundary marker of Israel (Acts 9:36–42).[^8] Here, the apostolic sign ministry achieves its climax: the total conquest of death through the raising of Tabitha.[^15][^53] This supreme miracle establishes Peter's absolute apostolic authority, setting the stage for the dramatic breakthrough of the gospel to the Gentiles in Caesarea under Acts 10.[^8]

## Theological Analyses of Apostolic Persecution in the Book of Acts

The record of apostolic persecution in the book of Acts presents a rich landscape for Reformed dogmatics, illustrating the intersections of divine sovereignty, human responsibility, covenantal transition, and the absolute authority of Jesus Christ. By examining the expositions of prominent Reformed theologians on Acts 4:1–21, Acts 5:17–42, and Acts 12:1–19, a systematic framework emerges. This framework addresses how the early church navigated the hostility of both religious and civil authorities.

The following table provides a comparative summary of the three pericopes, highlighting the primary sources of opposition, the specific theological themes developed by the surveyed theologians, and the key passages or concepts they emphasize.

| Pericope | Opposing Authority | Key Theological Emphasis | Primary Surveyed Source Materials |

| Acts 5:17–42 | High Priest and Sadducees [^58] | New Covenant spiritual transformation; rejection of predictable theological reductionism; joy as a sign of union with Christ [^58][^59] | Carson [^58][^59], Piper [^60], Spurgeon [^61], DeYoung [^62] |

## Acts 4:1–21: The Exclusivity of Christ and the Limits of Civil Allegiance

### Historical and Cultural Background

The setting of Acts 4 is the Temple precinct in Jerusalem, where Peter and John are arrested by the temple priests, the captain of the temple, and the Sadducees.[^54] R.C. Sproul explains that this group constituted the Sanhedrin, the supreme ecclesiastical court of Israel, dominated at the time by the high-priestly families of Annas and Caiaphas.[^54] The historical motive driving the Sadducees' intervention was their intense theological aversion to the doctrine of the resurrection from the dead, which the apostles preached in the name of Jesus.[^54]

Joel Beeke highlights the cultural significance of Acts 4:13, noting that the Sanhedrin was astonished by the boldness of Peter and John because they were "uneducated and untrained men" who had not attended the prestigious rabbinic schools.[^55] The court's ultimate recognition "that they had been with Jesus" indicates that apostolic authority did not derive from contemporary academic credentialing but from direct union with and instruction by Christ.[^55]

### Christological Exclusivity and the Polemic Against Pluralism

In his exposition of Acts 4:12, Sproul addresses the modern cultural offense taken at the biblical claim of Christ's exclusive salvific path.[^54] He cautions that if modern readers are offended by the narrowness of the text, they must examine their own hearts rather than project bias onto the first-century writers.[^54] Sproul traces a comprehensive redemptive-historical narrative to defend this exclusivity:

- God created humanity in His image, but humanity fell into cosmic treason, aligning with the Serpent.[^54]
- God was under no obligation to save anyone, yet He chose to clothe Adam and Eve, deliver Israel from Egypt, and give them the Law, which commanded absolute allegiance to Yahweh.[^54]
- Despite Israel stoning the prophets, God sent His only-begotten Son to take on human flesh and bear the full wrath of the cross.[^54]
- In light of this supreme sacrifice, Sproul argues that for humanity to demand multiple paths to heaven is an insult to the holiness of God.[^54]

To illustrate this, Sproul recounts a personal historical anecdote from his college years, where a hostile professor—a former war correspondent—publicly demanded to know if Sproul believed Jesus was the only way to God.[^54] Upon his affirmative response, the professor publicly ridiculed him as narrow-minded.[^54] Sproul argues that if Jesus is merely "a" way to God, but claims to be the "only" way (John 14:6), then He is a deceiver if other ways exist; thus, Christ is logically the only Savior, or He is no Savior at all.[^54]

J.I. Packer supports this rigid exclusivity, stating that "all truth is exclusive" by definition.[^57] He reinforces that sincerity is never the test of truth, as people can be sincerely wrong.[^57] Packer traces the absolute authority of the New Testament back to Christ's delegation of His own divine authority to the apostles.[^66] Because the apostles were promised the Holy Spirit to guide their speech and witness, their proclamation in Acts 4:12 serves as the unchanging norm for Christian faith throughout church history.[^66]

### Parameters of Civil Obedience and Resistance

John MacArthur analyzes the legal confrontation in Acts 4:18–20, where the Sanhedrin orders the apostles to cease preaching in the name of Jesus.[^56] MacArthur contrasts this text with Romans 13:3–5, which demands absolute subjection to civil authorities.[^56] He asserts that the apostolic response ("Whether it is right in the sight of God to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge") establishes the singular biblical exception for civil disobedience: when a human magistrate commands a believer to violate a direct command of God’s Word.[^56] MacArthur warns that while Christians must submit to civil structures under normal circumstances, they must be prepared to stand for the Lord and accept the punitive consequences of their disobedience when those structures demand spiritual treason.[^56]

To illuminate this resistance, MacArthur provides a detailed word study of the Greek term phimoo (from phimoomai), which Peter later employs in 1 Peter 2:15 to command believers to "silence the ignorance of foolish men".[^56] The term literally pictures "muzzling" an animal.[^56] MacArthur argues that the most effective tool to muzzle civil and cultural critics is not political rebellion, but a life characterized by consistent, godly submission and righteousness.[^56] The apostles in Acts 4 did not resist arrest or seek to overthrow the Sanhedrin; they submitted to physical custody while refusing to compromise their mandate to preach the gospel.[^56]

### Hermeneutical Safeguards

Sinclair Ferguson warns against a common hermeneutical error when reading narratives like Acts 4.[^67] He cautions believers against treating the text as a spiritual game of "Where’s Waldo?", wherein the reader's primary goal is to locate themselves in the narrative (e.g., imagining themselves as the bold Peter before the council).[^67] Ferguson argues that the primary focus of biblical narrative must always be Christological: "Who is Jesus here, and what is He doing?".[^67] Peter’s boldness is not an example of human courage to be moralistically copied, but a testimony to the ongoing, active reign of the resurrected Christ through the Holy Spirit.[^67]

### Documentation of Extant and Missing Sources

The theologians surveyed vary in their direct literary output on this pericope:

- **Sproul**: Detailed sermon "No Other Name" [^54] and "Holy Boldness" on Acts 4:23–31.[^68]
- **MacArthur**: Expository sermons and study guides "How to Handle Persecution, Part 1 & 2" (Acts 4:1–32).[^56][^69]
- **Packer**: Systematic treatments of Acts 4:12 [^57] and Christological authority.[^66]
- **Beeke**: Specific theological analysis of apostolic discipleship in Acts 4:13.[^55]
- **Ferguson**: General hermeneutical principles applying to Acts.[^67]
- **John Piper, D.A. Carson, Michael Horton, Kevin DeYoung, Alastair Begg, Charles Spurgeon**: No direct, verse-by-verse exposition of Acts 4:1–21 was discoverable within the collected primary source materials, although their systematic alignment with the doctrines of solus Christus and civil obedience is consistent with the broader Reformed tradition

## Acts 5:17–42: The Sanhedrin's Jealousy, New Covenant Power, and Gamaliel's Counsel

### Redemptive-Historical Typology: Old Covenant vs. New Covenant

D.A. Carson’s exposition of Acts 5:17–42 in his sermon "Facing the Foe" contrasts the structural operations of the Old and New Covenants.[^58] Carson explains that the Old Covenant was fundamentally a tribal, corporate arrangement.[^58] Under that administration, God dealt with the Israelite community as a whole through designated representative leaders (Moses, kings, priests) upon whom the Holy Spirit was uniquely poured out.[^58] If a representative leader sinned, the entire nation suffered the immediate consequences of covenantal chastisement.[^58]

In contrast, the New Covenant—prophesied in Jeremiah 31 and inaugurated at Pentecost—involves a radical, individual transformation of the entire community.[^58] The Holy Spirit is poured out upon all believers, writing the law directly on their minds and hearts.[^58] The boldness of the apostles in Acts 5:29 ("We must obey God rather than men") is not merely a display of personal willpower, but a direct consequence of this universal, New Covenant spiritual regeneration.[^58]

### Exegetical Warning Against Predictable Reductionism

Carson uses the miracles in Acts 5:12–16—specifically the healing of those on whom Peter's shadow fell—to issue a warning against theological reductionism.[^58] He defines this error as the human tendency to want God "taped, domesticated, [and] predictable".[^58] Carson demonstrates that God's sovereign actions cannot be reduced to a simplistic formula:

- While God chose to work extraordinary physical miracles through Peter's shadow in Jerusalem, He did not establish this as a perpetual, predictable mechanism for the church.[^58]
- In other New Testament contexts, God chose not to heal His servants: Paul left Trophimus sick in Miletus, experienced a personal "thorn in the flesh," and advised Timothy to take wine for his frequent stomach ailments.[^58]
- Kevin DeYoung supports this distinction, asserting that while physical miracles are not the ordinary, daily experience of the post-apostolic era, God remains entirely sovereign to act as He pleases.[^62] DeYoung argues that true faith does not demand that God conform to human rational expectations, but submits to a Creator who reserves the right to suspend natural laws according to His own decree.[^62]

### The Sadducean Mindset and Gamaliel's Strategy

Charles Spurgeon, writing in Morning & Evening, provides historical analysis of the Sadducees who arrested the apostles.[^61] He designates them as the "Broad Churchmen of their day," noting the historical irony that their self-proclaimed theological tolerance did not prevent them from becoming the most vicious persecutors of apostolic truth.[^61] Spurgeon remarks that "men of no religion are frequently the greatest bigots in the world," driven to fury when their self-righteous systems are threatened by the simple proclamation of the gospel.[^61]

Carson and Spurgeon offer distinct assessments of Gamaliel’s famous defense of the apostles in Acts 5:34–39:

- **Spurgeon** analyzes Gamaliel as a worldly, calculating, and "prudent" politician who advocated for religious toleration purely for policy's sake.[^61] He observes that the Lord frequently raises up such secular protectors within the enemy’s own camp to preserve the church from destruction.[^61]
- **Carson** focuses on the political and theological divisions within the Sanhedrin.[^58] Because the Pharisees (Gamaliel’s party) held a dogmatic defense of the resurrection, and the Sadducees (the high priest's party) rejected it, Gamaliel strategically exploited this division.[^58] By warning the court that executing the apostles might result in "fighting against God," Gamaliel effectively paralyzed the Sadducees' immediate legal authority to impose the death penalty.[^58] Carson asserts that God, in His providence, uses such varied human degrees and political divisions to limit and control the suffering imposed upon His church.[^58]

### Joy in Suffering and the Meaning of Scourging

Carson's sermon "Rejoice to Suffer for the Name" examines the reality of the physical scourging the apostles received in Acts 5:40.[^59] He emphasizes that apostolic joy (Acts 5:41) was not a form of psychological masochism; the apostles did not enjoy the physical pain of the whip.[^59] Rather, their joy was grounded in the reality that they were "counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name".[^59] Carson argues that suffering for Christ's sake serves as a profound confirmation of a believer's union with Jesus, connecting them to the historical line of true prophets and saints who faced similar hostility from a fallen world.[^59]

John Piper reinforces this in his message on Christian humility and cultural conflict, arguing that godly conviction, far from being arrogant, is characterized by a durable, serious joy that enables believers to endure physical abuse and social ostracization without retaliating.[^60][^70] Piper asserts that a self-exalting pursuit of human approval is fundamentally incompatible with saving faith in a crucified Messiah.[^71]

### Documentation of Extant and Missing Sources

- **Carson**: Detailed sermons "Facing the Foe" [^58] and "Rejoice to Suffer for the Name".[^59]
- **Piper**: Multiple expository messages on Acts 5, including "The Price and the Preciousness of Spiritual Power" (Acts 5:17–21), "Obey the Government for God’s Sake" (Acts 5:29), and "If It Is of God, It Will Not Be Overthrown" (Acts 5:33–42).[^60][^70]
- **Spurgeon**: Expositions in his daily devotional material.[^61]
- **DeYoung**: Expository articles on Acts 5:12–16 [^62] and the sufficiency of the Word.[^72]
- **MacArthur**: References to Acts 5:29 as a boundary for civil submission [^56] and expositions of Acts 5:12–16.[^73]
- **Sproul**: Discusses the adjacent incident of Ananias and Sapphira in Acts 5:1–11 in his series on the holiness of God [^74], but has no direct, discoverable primary exposition of Acts 5:17–42.
- **Packer, Ferguson, Beeke, Horton, Begg**: No direct expository works on Acts 5:17–42 were discoverable within the primary source materials

## Acts 12:1–19: Sovereign Providence, Martyrdom, and the Illusion of Human Power

### Historical and Political Context

The historical background of Acts 12 is dominated by the political career of King Herod Agrippa I.[^63] Sproul provides a detailed historical reconstruction of Agrippa's rise to power:

- Agrippa was the grandson of Herod the Great (who ordered the slaughter of the infants in Bethlehem).[^63]
- Following the execution of his father Aristobulus in 7 BC, the young Agrippa was sent to Rome for his safety, where he was raised in the imperial household alongside the future emperors Caligula and Claudius.[^63]
- Upon Caligula’s accession in AD 37, Agrippa was granted kingship over Galilee, and under Claudius, his territory was expanded to include Judea and Samaria.[^63]
- This expansion replaced the direct rule of Roman procurators (such as Pontius Pilate), positioning Agrippa as a client king over the entire region.[^63]
Sproul explains that Agrippa's persecution of the Jerusalem church was a calculated political maneuver designed to please the local Jewish population and secure his own throne.[^63] To demonstrate his commitment to traditional Judaism, he executed James, the brother of John, with the sword, and subsequently arrested Peter during the Days of Unleavened Bread, intending to execute him after the Passover feast.[^63]

### The Providential Mystery of Martyrdom and Rescue

John Piper, in his article "One Believer Dies, Another Lives: On Reading Providence," addresses the profound theological problem presented by the opening of Acts 12: why did God allow James to be executed while choosing to miraculously deliver Peter?.[^64] Piper highlights the stark narrative contrast: the martyrdom of James is recorded in a brief, matter-of-fact sentence (Acts 12:2), while the supernatural rescue of Peter is detailed over twelve verses.[^64]

Piper argues that the point of this contrast is to show that both outcomes were entirely within the sovereign decree of God [^64]:

- James’s execution was not a failure of divine protection, but the fulfillment of Christ’s specific prophecy in Matthew 20:23, where Jesus told James and John that they would indeed "drink the cup" of His suffering.[^64][^75] Herod was not acting outside of God’s control; he was the human instrument executing the pre-determined counsel of God.[^64]
- Peter’s rescue, conversely, was designed to expose the utter futility of human pride.[^64] Luke details Herod’s extreme security measures—the chains, the sixteen guards, and the iron gates—to make Herod’s plans look foolish.[^64] When the angel of the Lord appeared, the chains fell off effortlessly, and the gates opened "of their own accord," proving that no earthly potentate can bind what God intends to loose.[^64]

### The Critique of Prosperity Theology and the Irony of Unbelief

Sproul uses the contrasting fates of James and Peter to dismantle modern prosperity ("word-of-faith") theology.[^63] He notes that prosperity teachers claim that a failure to receive physical healing or deliverance from suffering is due to a lack of faith.[^63] Sproul argues that both James and Peter were apostles of extraordinary faith, yet one was beheaded and the other was freed.[^63] This demonstrates that faith is not a mechanism to manipulate God, but a posture of absolute submission to His sovereign will, which includes both suffering and deliverance.[^63]

Sproul also points out the deep irony of the prayer meeting at the house of Mary (Acts 12:12–16).[^63] While the church was gathered to offer "constant prayer" for Peter’s release, they utterly refused to believe Rhoda when she announced that Peter was standing at the gate.[^63] They accused her of being "beside herself" and concluded that it must be "his angel".[^63] Sproul labels this "an astonishment born not of faith but of unbelief," warning modern believers not to treat prayer as a magic formula or to view God as a "cosmic bellhop" who is bound to do our bidding.[^63]

### The Fall of the Tyrant and the Sovereign Word

Kevin DeYoung and Sproul examine the immediate sequel to Peter’s rescue: the death of Herod Agrippa I in Caesarea (Acts 12:20–23).[^65][^76] Sproul provides historical context for Agrippa’s dispute with the Phoenician cities of Tyre and Sidon, explaining that Agrippa had cut off their grain supply from Galilee.[^76] He notes the economic reality that "when goods and services freely cross borders, soldiers rarely do," showing that Agrippa used economic warfare to force the Phoenicians to sue for peace through Blastus, the king's chamberlain.[^76]

On the set day of the Phoenician delegation’s audience, Agrippa arrayed himself in royal silver apparel and delivered an oration.[^76] When the crowd shouted that his voice was "the voice of a god and not of a man," Agrippa accepted the praise.[^65][^76] DeYoung notes that Agrippa committed no overt crime against humanity in that specific moment, but was immediately struck down by an angel because "he did not give God the glory".[^65] He was eaten by worms and died.[^76] DeYoung uses this to warn ministers and leaders against the peril of absorbing human praise rather than immediately redirecting all glory to God.[^65] Piper notes that the chapter begins with Herod killing an apostle and ends with an angel killing Herod, demonstrating that "if you oppose Jesus, you lose".[^71] Despite Herod’s violence, the narrative closes with the statement: "But the word of God increased and multiplied".[^71][^72]

### Cross-Pericope Narrative Progression and Doctrinal Synthesis

When these three accounts of persecution are evaluated together, the theologians trace a distinct narrative progression in both the severity of the opposition and the corresponding manifestation of divine sovereignty.

This structural and thematic progression is illustrated in the table below, mapping the escalation of the conflict from local religious warnings to imperial capital punishment.

| Pericope | Level of Authority | Nature of Persecution | Divine Response / Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acts 4:1–21 | Local Sanhedrin (Saddcuees) [^54] | Verbal warnings and legal threats [^54][^68] | Great boldness in preaching; church growth to 5,000 [^54][^68] |
| Acts 5:17–42 | National Sanhedrin (High Priest) [^58] | Public imprisonment and physical scourging [^58][^61] | Angelic prison release; political paralysis via Gamaliel; joy in suffering [^58][^61] |
| Acts 12:1–19 | Imperial Client King (Herod Agrippa I) [^63] | Beheading of James; maximum-security imprisonment of Peter [^63][^64] | Miraculous escape; immediate execution of the tyrant; multiplying of the Word [^71][^72] |

### The Progression of Hostility and Sovereignty

The level of opposition escalates through a clear progression:

- The persecution begins in Acts 4 as a local, verbal warning from the religious authorities, who are legally constrained from taking further action due to public opinion.[^54][^68]
- In Acts 5, the hostility escalates to physical violence, as the high priest and Sadducees administer a public scourging to the apostles before releasing them with further threats.[^58][^61]
- In Acts 12, the persecution shifts from local religious leaders to the civil imperial authority of Herod Agrippa I, resulting in the capital execution of James and the maximum-security imprisonment of Peter.[^63][^64]

In response to this escalating human opposition, the display of divine sovereignty becomes increasingly dramatic:

- In Acts 4, God answers the church’s prayer for boldness by shaking the building and filling them with the Holy Spirit to continue their public witness.[^68]
- In Acts 5, God bypasses the physical prison guards, sending an angel to open the locked doors and ordering the apostles to return to the temple courts.[^58] He then uses the internal Pharisaic-Sadducean divide to protect them from immediate execution.[^58]
- In Acts 12, God allows one apostle to be martyred while delivering another past sixteen guards and iron gates, ultimately striking down the persecuting king in a public display of immediate judgment.[^63][^64]

### The Sufficiency and Triumph of the Word

The common thread linking these three narratives is the absolute invincibility of the Word of God under persecution.[^71][^72] As DeYoung and Piper summarize, human magistrates and laws may restrain the physical bodies of the apostles, but they cannot bind the gospel.[^71][^72] In each instance of persecution, the narrative concludes with the failure of the persecutors and the growth of the church, demonstrating that the word of God is sufficient to do the work of God, and that the sovereign Lord reigns supreme over the designs of kings and rulers.[^64][^68][^72]
